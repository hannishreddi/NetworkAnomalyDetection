What the project is for

The problem

Most network monitors watch one number, usually traffic volume, and alarm when it crosses a line. That misses attacks that do not add much traffic, such as a port scan or a sudden shift from TCP to UDP. Fixed baselines also go stale as normal traffic slowly drifts over days.

The question

Can a training-free statistical detector catch the anomalies that single-feature volume monitors miss, keep false alarms low, and stay accurate when normal traffic drifts, at a cost light enough to run continuously?

The answer

Scoring several traffic features together is what defeats non-volumetric attacks, in simulation and on real traffic. Online adaptation on top of that ties the static model when traffic is steady and wins when traffic drifts, which is the realistic case for a monitor left running.

Each window becomes a feature vector: log packets, log bytes, unique destination ports and unique source IPs, the SYN ratio and UDP fraction, plus log packets-per-source and log bytes-per-packet. The two ratio features are what expose attacks that do not change volume.

The detector keeps an exponentially weighted (EWMA) mean m and covariance S of that vector and scores each window by its normalised Mahalanobis distance. When a window looks suspicious, the mean only creeps and the covariance freezes, so an attack cannot teach the model that it is normal.

Six detectors are compared on the same window → feature → detector pipeline: four conventional baselines (fixed threshold, moving average, z-score, static Mahalanobis), one heavier machine-learning reference (Isolation Forest), and the proposed adaptive detector. Hyper-parameters and thresholds were tuned only on development seeds 100–109; the reported numbers come from held-out seeds the method never saw. On real data the method is frozen and only the alarm threshold is calibrated on a benign period.

Event F1 combines event recall (an attack counts as caught if any alarm fires during it) with alarm-episode precision (a run of alarms is a false alarm if it overlaps no attack).

Results on simulated traffic

The simulator produces window-level traffic with daily cycles, random wander, benign flash crowds and five labelled attack families. Each figure averages 30 held-out traces; whiskers are 95% confidence intervals.

Mixed attacks: event F1 by detector

The two multivariate detectors clear every single-feature baseline by a wide margin. Adaptive and static Mahalanobis are statistically tied here.

Robustness to drifting normal traffic

As benign traffic drifts further from where it started, static detectors raise more false alarms and lose F1. The adaptive detector stays almost flat.

Event recall by attack type

Port scans and protocol shifts are caught only by the multivariate detectors. Slow ramps are the adaptive detector's weak point: its own adaptation partly absorbs a gradual change.

Results on real traffic (CIC-IDS2017)

The same detectors, frozen, run on per-minute windows built from the Friday port-scan and DDoS captures of CIC-IDS2017. Thresholds are set on benign minutes so that roughly 5% of benign minutes alarm.

Share of attack minutes caught

The simulated finding reappears: multivariate scoring catches the non-volumetric port scan that volume monitors miss. DDoS is an easy volumetric case for most detectors.

Port-scan capture, minute by minute

Shaded minutes are labelled attack. Scan minutes stand out in unique destination ports, while their packet counts overlap with ordinary busy minutes. No single feature separates them cleanly, which is why the detectors score all features together.

Limitations

  • The controlled experiments use synthetic traffic, so absolute numbers reflect the simulator's assumptions rather than a specific production network.
  • The CIC-IDS2017 captures are single, hour-long sessions at minute resolution: too short and too steady to show the drift advantage on real traffic.
  • The detector trails on very slow-ramping attacks, because its own adaptation partly absorbs a gradual change.
